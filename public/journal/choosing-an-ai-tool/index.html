Buyer's guide · Field note 002

How to choose an AI tool without paying for complexity.

The best tool is not necessarily the one with the most features. It is the one that reliably improves a real workflow without adding more work than it removes.

TeamPeach editorial5 minute readPractical selection guide

AI products can make similar promises while solving very different problems. Some are good at drafting. Others connect systems, classify incoming information, or take a sequence of actions. Buying before defining the task makes it easy to pay for capability you will never use.

1. Write down the task before the tool

Describe the job in plain language. Who initiates it? What information is available? What should the result look like? What happens after that result is produced?

Try a simple before-and-after statement: “Today, our team spends 40 minutes reviewing incoming requests and copying the key facts into a tracker. We need a first draft of that summary, with a person checking it before it is saved.” That is much more actionable than “We need AI for operations.”

2. Measure the useful outcome

Pick one or two metrics that matter: time saved per case, fewer errors, shorter response time, better consistency, or higher completion rate. Decide how you will measure the current process before changing it.

A convincing demo is not the same thing as a reliable improvement in everyday work.

3. Choose the least complicated fit

  • Use a focused tool when one well-defined task—such as rewriting, summarising or extracting fields—is the main need.
  • Use a workflow automation when the steps are predictable and systems need to pass data to one another.
  • Consider an AI agent when the work involves interpreting context, choosing among steps and using tools with appropriate safeguards.
  • Build a custom solution when your process, data or controls are not supported well by an off-the-shelf product.

4. Check the limits and the real cost

Ask what the product can access, what it stores, where a human needs to review the result, how failures are handled, and whether you can export your data. For paid services, understand the subscription, usage limits, credits, integration work and support costs—not just the headline price.

Test it against representative real examples, including awkward edge cases. Check false positives, missing details, formatting and whether users can tell when the system is uncertain.

5. Run a small pilot with a stop rule

Choose one team, one task and a defined trial period. Agree on what success looks like and what would cause you to stop or redesign the approach. Keep a human review step for decisions with meaningful consequences.

When you can show repeatable value on a small scale, expand carefully. If the result still depends on constant manual repair, another feature may not be the answer; the task or workflow may need to be simplified first.

Questions to answer before buying

  1. What exact job will this tool do, and what will it not do?
  2. How will we know it is better than the current process?
  3. What data and permissions does it need?
  4. How much does it cost at our expected usage?
  5. How can a person review, correct or undo its work?

Start with the work, not the feature list. That is usually the shortest route to a tool people will actually keep using.
