Operations · Field note 003

Business tasks worth considering for automation.

Start with work that repeats, follows clear rules and produces a checkable result. Automate the handoff—not judgement that should remain human.

TeamPeach editorial7 minute readWorkflow guide

Most businesses have small, repetitive tasks that quietly consume time: copying information between systems, reminding someone about a missing field, preparing a recurring report or routing requests to the right person. These are often better starting points for automation than a broad project to “automate the whole business.”

Look for repetition and a clear handoff

A promising task happens frequently, follows a pattern and ends with an output that can be checked. It should also have a known owner and a clear exception path.

Good places to look first

1. Intake and classification

Collect incoming enquiries, application forms or support requests, check for missing information, categorise them and route them to the right queue. Let a person handle ambiguous or sensitive cases.

2. Document extraction and preparation

Extract fields from invoices, receipts, forms or meeting notes into a structured draft. Validate required fields and preserve a link to the original document so a person can verify the result.

3. Follow-ups and reminders

Trigger reminders when a known condition is met: a form is incomplete, a deadline is approaching or a handoff has not been acknowledged. Keep message frequency sensible and provide a way to stop or correct reminders.

4. Recurring reports

Gather information from agreed sources, reconcile totals, highlight changes and draft a report. Automate data collection first; require review where context is needed to interpret unusual changes.

5. Content and communication drafts

Prepare a first draft from approved inputs, adapt it for a channel or format, and check that mandatory details are present. Keep an approval step before publication or sending messages externally.

6. Internal knowledge lookup

Help staff find approved policies, procedures or product details. Use source links and access controls, and make it easy to report an incomplete or outdated answer.

Calculate value before building

Estimate how much time the task consumes each week, how often rework happens and how costly an error would be. Include setup, integration, monitoring and the time needed to review results. A process that takes only a few minutes but creates serious risk may not be a good candidate.

Automate the repeatable steps; make exceptions visible; keep a person accountable for the outcome.

Where automation should stop

Be careful with decisions affecting money, employment, legal obligations, health, safety or access to essential services. Automation may assist with gathering information and presenting options, but meaningful decisions need appropriate oversight, clear accountability and a way to challenge errors.

Also examine the process before automating it. Repetitive work sometimes exists because a form asks the wrong questions or two systems collect the same information. Removing a bad step can be simpler than creating a bot to perform it faster.

Run a controlled trial

  1. Map the current steps and identify the owner.
  2. Choose one measurable improvement.
  3. Test normal cases and realistic exceptions.
  4. Decide who reviews errors and what happens when a system is unavailable.
  5. Compare the result with the old process before expanding.

Good automation should make the work clearer and easier to control. If it creates a hidden process nobody can explain or correct, it is not finished yet.
